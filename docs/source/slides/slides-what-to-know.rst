
What to know about slide decks
=====================================

Concept 01: A moving, visual document
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Slides are a combination of **text**, **graphic design**, and **motion graphics**.

* Including **text** means that slide decks use the **same elements as documents**, including:  

  * Titles  
  * Headings  
  * Body text  
  * Bullet points  
  * Table of contents  
  * Internal links

\[image of graphic design poster\]

* Including **graphic design** means that slide decks use the **same elements as static visual media** such as posters, logos, magazine layouts, product packaging, and websites. These elements include:  

  * Lines  
  * Shapes  
  * Textures  
  * Spacing  
  * Colours  
  * Fonts

\[clip of video with motion graphics\]

* Including motion graphics means that slide decks use the same elements as videos, including:  

  * Animations  
  * Transitions  
  * E.g.: Animated text and shapes in videos

Concept 02: Content vs. layouts
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

In a slide deck, slide **content** and slide **layouts** are *stored*
separately.

-  Content is the text and multimedia items on the slides.

   -  Slide design software allows you to view the content of the slide
      deck as plain text.

-  Layouts determine the visual appearance and arrangement of the
   content on the slides.

   -  Each layout provides intended spaces, called placeholders, for
      various types of content to be placed in.

Concept 03: Layouts, themes, & structured design
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

In good design work, slide content and the layout of that content are
*designed* separately.

-  In a well made slide deck, each slide is based on an existing layout,
   and each slide with the same layout has the same appearance.

   -  This mirrors the concept of **structured authoring** discussed in
      the Documents module.

-  Treating content and layouts separately allows for much greater
   flexibility.

   -  The design of individual layouts or all layouts can be changed
      even after content has been added.
   -  Themes can be easily swapped to change the appearance of the
      entire slide deck.

-  When using external themes (such as those downloaded from online
   templates), open the layout editor to see how the theme’s slides are
   structured.

   -  If the appearance of the slides doesn’t match the appearance of
      the layouts, then the theme is poorly designed and shouldn’t be
      used.

Tip: How to maintain greatest flexibility in your designs by keeping
slide content and slide layout separate

-  When adding content

   -  When creating a new slide, insert an appropriate layout from the
      layout menu
   -  When adding content to a slide, add content into the spots
      provided by the layout
   -  If none of the existing layouts contain appropriate placeholders
      for the content you have or have the appearance that you want,
      first edit the slide deck’s theme to create a layout that suits
      your design.

-  Bad ideas:

   -  When creating a new slide, copy a slide you already made and then
      move the elements around.
   -  When adding content to a slide, draw a new text box

.. note:: Question: "What’s the point of doing this?"

   Fair question! Let’s address some specific complaints.

   -  "It’s faster to just move items around and drag in images. Making a
   new layout for each new type of slide takes too long."

      -  Making a new layout does take more time upfront. However, once
         you’re familiar with the process of adding and editing layouts,
         it’s just a few extra seconds.

   -  "I know I’m never going to change the design of my slide deck later."

      -  Are you sure about that? If you’re designing as part of a team,
         the other members may want to change what you’ve designed. If
         you’re designing an important slide deck for a boss or a client,
         they will definitely ask you to make changes to the content and to
         the design. Good design is an iterative process; it’s best to
         expect that and work in a way that makes future changes easier.

   -  "My slide deck has just a few slides, like fewer than 10."

      -  It’s less about the amount of slides and more about the complexity
         of the slides. If you spend 20 - 30 minutes perfecting the
         appearance of one slide, do you really want to repeat that process
         for each of the other similar slides? Simply copying and pasting
         an existing slide doesn’t always work out smoothly if there are
         many individual elements to revise.

   -  "Why does everything need to have a consistent appearance? The
      audience won’t notice or care."

      -  They will notice, and they will care. Readers who have seen many
         slide decks (like your teachers or your bosses) will consciously
         notice the design problems. Less experienced readers will
         subconsciously notice the problems and feel that the slides look
         “sloppy”, even if they can’t immediately explain why.
      -  A common example: One type of poor problem is the “floating
         debris” slide deck. In these slide decks, the content appears to
         slightly drift a little bit in random directions when moving
         between slides. This happens because the designer manually created
         each slide’s layout instead of using an existing layout.
         [add example]

   At the end of the day, the purpose of this is to save you time and
   headaches.

   -  A little bit of extra work up front can save you a lot of time later
      once you’re ready to begin revising your slides.
   -  If editing is quick and easy, you’re more likely to do it and try out
      more types of designs and end up with a better result. (If editing is
      slow and hard (and repetitive and annoying), then you’re less likely
      to do it. You’re likely to end up with a result that you don’t like
      but can’t easily change.)

Concept 04: Primary resource vs. secondary resource
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Slide decks can be designed as a visual document or supporting materials
for a presentation.

If the audience is expected to view the slide deck on their own time,
then the slide deck is a type of highly visual document. It is the
primary resource for the information it contains.

-  As a primary resource, a slide deck should contain all information
   the audience needs.
-  The audience does not have easy access to the designer to ask
   questions about the content, similar to other kinds of documents.

If the audience is expected to view the slides as part of a live or
recorded presentation, then the slide is a type of supporting material.
It is a secondary resource for the information it contains.

-  As a secondary resource, a slide deck should contain only essential
   information, such as keywords, important figures, images, or charts.

   -  If a slide is dense with text and information, the audience will
      either listen to the speaker or read the slide. They cannot do
      both at the same time.

-  Detailed information comes from the presenter’s speaking. The
   audience can ask for details and clarifications, if needed.
-  The slides should be easy to view and understand, and not distract
   from the speaker’s verbal message.

Some slide decks are meant to be both a primary resource and a secondary
resource.

-  Be careful about putting too much text or information on any given
   slide.

   -  If a slide is dense with text and information, the audience will
      either listen to the speaker or read the slide. They cannot do
      both at the same time.

-  Consider placing any excess information in a separate document, or in
   hidden slides that you don’t show during the live presentation.

^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Slide design is a type of graphic design. Graphic design principles help
us design better slides.

-  Good design enhances the message, and is usually ‘invisible’. Bad
   design harms the message by distracting the reader’s attention.
